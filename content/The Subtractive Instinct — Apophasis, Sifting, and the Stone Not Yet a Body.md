There's a story about Michelangelo that isn't quite true, or at least isn't provably true, and I want to start there instead of pretending otherwise. The line goes: he saw the angel in the marble and carved until he set it free. It's a beautiful sentence. It circulates constantly — in sermons, in coaching decks, in the kind of LinkedIn post that gets the idea half right. And as far as anyone can trace, it doesn't appear anywhere in his own letters or verse in that clean a form ([Wikiquote lists it among the unsourced attributions](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Michelangelo)). What Michelangelo actually wrote about was looser — the *concetto*, the idea a sculptor's hand serves in bringing something out of stone. Related, but not quite the same claim. The tidy version got added on, after the fact, to a man whose actual practice was subtraction.

I find that a little funny and a little instructive. Even our stories about taking things away tend to get built up.

Here's the idea underneath the misquote, though, and I keep running into it from enough different directions that I've stopped believing it's a coincidence: some kinds of work aren't creation so much as removal. Something is already there — the form the stone was always going to become, the truth a joke gets at sideways instead of head-on, the clarity waiting underneath everything you didn't need to say — and the actual labor is clearing it, not building it.

Theology has a name for this, and it's older and more rigorous than the sculpture story. [Pseudo-Dionysius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-dionysius-areopagite/), writing sometime in the fifth or sixth century, argued that God exceeds every predicate we could pin on him, so the truest speech about God has to proceed by stripping language away rather than piling it on — the *via negativa*, the negative way: you get closer by unsaying. Meister Eckhart takes the same move further: subtract every attribute, he says, including the word "God" as most people mean it, and only then are you near anything real. A scholar named William Franke has traced this lineage past its usual stopping point, to a fifth-century philosopher named Damascius, who negated even the negation — and made an argument I haven't been able to put down since: that modern philosophy's rejection of theology is, structurally, still a form of negative theology. You don't get to exit the shape by refusing the content. The unsaying is still a kind of saying.

Discernment carries the same instinct in its bones, literally, in its etymology — it comes from the Greek for "to separate," "to sift through." Discernment was never the accumulation of more information until an answer surfaces. It's the removal of what doesn't belong until what's left is legible. Which means the premature answer — reaching for a conclusion before the sifting is finished — isn't a shortcut to clarity. It's an additive failure wearing clarity's clothes.

There's a cosmological version of the same fork too, and it's the one I find myself living inside more than the others: does God add being to a void, or draw out what a formless deep already, generatively, held? The theologian Catherine Keller calls the second version *creatio ex profundis* — creation out of the deep — set deliberately against the more familiar *creatio ex nihilo*, creation out of nothing. I land on the deep-side of that fork more often than not. Nothing needed to be added. The deep already held what would become.

I want to be honest about where this pattern strains, because a strong resemblance across four or five domains isn't the same claim as one proven truth underneath all of them. The clearest place I've watched it strain is in my own working process, not in anyone else's theology. Sculpture is subtractive in a way almost nothing else gets to be — you can't add-and-revise your way through a block of stone; the medium forecloses that. But even there, the actual discipline needs both things at once: enough stillness to sense what's already latent in the material, and enough nerve to start cutting before the whole shape is clear in your head. That's not a purely subtractive posture. It's subtraction that requires beginning before you're sure — which is, structurally, an additive act. The pattern is real. It just isn't as clean as I'd like it to be, and I trust it more for admitting that.

Where I actually feel this most these days is in composing again after years away from it — working from a dense field of sound and taking away, rather than building up note by note from silence. I don't play piano, which used to feel like a disqualifying fact about the kind of composer I could be. I've started to wonder if it isn't, instead, exactly the right starting condition for this particular way of working. You can't add your way through a field you can't play. You can only listen into it and start removing what isn't the piece.
